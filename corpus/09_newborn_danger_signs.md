# Newborn danger signs

The first days and weeks of a baby's life carry high risk in Sub-Saharan Africa,
where severe infection (neonatal sepsis) is a leading cause of newborn death.
Many newborn deaths happen at home, so a health worker or mother who can
recognise danger signs early and seek care quickly can save the baby's life. When
referral is not immediately possible, the priority is still to reach skilled care
as fast as possible.

## Danger signs in a newborn

Any of the following signs in a newborn is an emergency and requires urgent
referral:

The baby is unable to breastfeed or has stopped feeding well. The baby is
convulsing or having fits. The baby is breathing very fast, or has severe
in-drawing of the lower chest, or is grunting or struggling to breathe. The baby
is unusually sleepy, floppy, or difficult to wake (lethargy). The baby feels very
hot (fever) or unusually cold (low temperature) — both are danger signs in a
newborn. The baby's whole body or the palms and soles look yellow (jaundice),
especially in the first day or after the third day. There is redness, swelling,
or pus around the umbilical cord, or the baby's skin has blisters or many pustules.
The baby has stopped passing urine, has repeated vomiting, or has a swollen belly.

## Higher risk after certain births

A baby is at higher risk of infection if the waters broke a long time before birth
(more than about eighteen hours), if the mother had a fever around delivery, if
the labour was prolonged or obstructed, or if the baby was born early or very
small. These babies need closer watching for the danger signs above.

## What the health worker should do

Treat any newborn danger sign as an emergency requiring urgent referral to
skilled care. Encourage keeping the baby warm, continued breastfeeding where the
baby is able, clean cord care, and immediate care-seeking if any danger sign
appears. The assistant recognises the danger signs and advises urgent referral;
it does not diagnose or prescribe.
