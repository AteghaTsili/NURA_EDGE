# Malaria in pregnancy

In much of Sub-Saharan Africa, malaria is a major danger during pregnancy and a
leading contributor to maternal anaemia, miscarriage, stillbirth, premature
delivery, and low birth weight. Pregnancy lowers a woman's immunity to malaria,
so pregnant women are more likely to become infected and more likely to become
seriously ill. Recognising and acting on malaria early protects both mother and
baby.

## Warning signs of malaria in a pregnant woman

Fever, chills, headache, body aches, and tiredness are common signs. In a
pregnant woman, any fever should be taken seriously and assessed promptly, because
malaria can worsen quickly and cause severe anaemia.

Danger signs that require urgent referral include fever with the woman too weak
to stand or care for herself, convulsions, confusion or unusual drowsiness,
repeated vomiting so she cannot keep fluids down, very pale palms or inner eyelids
(a sign of severe anaemia), and difficulty breathing. These can indicate severe
malaria, which is a life-threatening emergency.

## Malaria and anaemia

Malaria destroys red blood cells and, combined with poor nutrition, frequently
causes anaemia in pregnancy — most severely between about 16 and 29 weeks. Severe
anaemia raises the risk of heart failure, dangerous bleeding at delivery, and
death. Signs of severe anaemia include very pale palms, inner eyelids, and
tongue, extreme tiredness, dizziness, fast heartbeat, and breathlessness on mild
exertion. A woman with these signs should be referred for assessment.

## What the health worker should do

Treat any fever in a pregnant woman as a reason for assessment, and treat the
danger signs above as reasons for urgent referral. Prevention through antenatal
care — including insecticide-treated bed nets and the preventive treatment
offered at antenatal visits — reduces this risk, so encourage early and regular
antenatal attendance. The assistant does not prescribe medication; its role is to
recognise the warning signs and ensure the woman reaches care in time.
