# Bleeding, miscarriage, and pregnancy loss

## Assessing vaginal bleeding

Vaginal bleeding in pregnancy is always a reason for assessment. The amount and
pattern help judge urgency. Heavy bleeding is a danger sign: soaking more than
one pad per hour for two or more hours, or passing large clots, requires urgent
referral to a health facility. Bleeding that is getting heavier over time, rather
than settling, is also concerning.

## Miscarriage

Miscarriage is common; roughly one in five recognised pregnancies ends in loss.
Bleeding and cramping in early pregnancy may indicate a miscarriage, but similar
symptoms can also occur with an ectopic pregnancy, so any woman with these
symptoms should be assessed.

After a miscarriage, heavy or continuing bleeding can indicate retained products
of conception, where tissue remains in the uterus. This is not something to be
managed at home and needs clinical assessment.

## Ectopic pregnancy

An ectopic pregnancy, where the pregnancy grows outside the uterus, is a
life-threatening emergency. Warning signs include severe abdominal pain,
one-sided lower abdominal pain, shoulder-tip pain, dizziness, or fainting in
early pregnancy. A woman with these signs must be referred immediately, even if
bleeding is light or absent.

## What the assistant must never do

An automated assistant must never confirm that a pregnancy loss has occurred.
Only a clinician can confirm a miscarriage or a completed loss, after
examination. The assistant's role is to recognise danger signs, advise referral,
and support the woman — not to make the diagnosis.

## After a confirmed loss: physical follow-up

Once a clinician has confirmed an uncomplicated early loss, routine follow-up
usually includes checking that bleeding is settling, that there is no fever, and
that the woman feels supported. Any return of heavy bleeding, fever, or severe
pain after a loss should prompt urgent reassessment, as these can signal
infection or retained tissue.
